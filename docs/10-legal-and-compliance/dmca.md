# DMCA & Takedowns

The **Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA)** is a US law best known for its
**notice‑and‑takedown** system and its **safe harbor** that shields online
services from liability for users' copyright infringement — *if* they follow the
rules. Even outside the US, similar notice‑and‑takedown regimes exist (e.g., the
EU), so the concepts here are broadly useful.

> ⚖️ Not legal advice. Copyright enforcement and safe‑harbor eligibility are
> fact‑specific; get professional advice for real disputes.

## Why it matters to you

Two situations bring the DMCA into your world:

1. **You host content others provide** (user comments, uploads, a forum, a
   marketplace) — you may want **safe‑harbor protection** from liability for what
   users post.
2. **Your content is infringed** by someone else online — you can send a
   **takedown notice** to get it removed.

And the flip side: **you receive a takedown notice** about content on your site
and must respond.

## Safe harbor (for services hosting user content)

The DMCA can protect a service provider from liability for **users'** infringing
content, provided it meets conditions, including:

- **Designating an agent** to receive copyright notices (and registering/publishing
  the agent's contact details).
- Having a **notice‑and‑takedown policy** and acting **expeditiously** to remove
  or disable access to infringing material upon a valid notice.
- **Not having actual knowledge** of infringement (and not ignoring obvious
  signs), and not profiting from infringement it can control.
- Adopting and reasonably implementing a **repeat‑infringer policy** (terminating
  users who repeatedly infringe).

> ✅ **Do:** If you host user‑generated content, set up a takedown process and
> (for US safe harbor) designate an agent, publish contact details, and adopt a
> repeat‑infringer policy. Address this in your
> [terms of service](terms-of-service.md).

## The takedown flow

1. A rights holder sends a **DMCA notice** (identifying the work, the infringing
   material's location, their contact info, and required statements/signature).
2. The service **removes or disables** the identified material expeditiously and
   notifies the user who posted it.
3. The user may file a **counter‑notice** asserting the material is
   non‑infringing (e.g., they own it, or it's authorized/fair use).
4. If a valid counter‑notice is filed, the service may **restore** the material
   after a waiting period, **unless** the rights holder files suit.

The service acts as a neutral intermediary following the process — it's not
adjudicating who's right.

## If you receive a takedown notice (about your own content)

> ✅ **Do:**
> - **Take it seriously** and act promptly — ignoring it risks liability and
>   account/host action.
> - **Assess validity** — is the claim legitimate? Do you actually have the right
>   to the content? (Check your license records — see
>   [copyright & IP](copyright-and-ip.md).)
> - If you're infringing: **remove or properly license** the content.
> - If you believe you're **not** infringing (you own it, it's licensed, or it's
>   a clear fair‑use/exception case): consider a **counter‑notice** — but
>   understand that a counter‑notice can expose you to a lawsuit, so weigh it
>   carefully, ideally with legal advice.

> ❌ **Don't:**
> - File a **false counter‑notice** — misrepresentations carry legal
>   consequences.
> - Ignore notices and hope they go away.

## Guard against abuse (in both directions)

- **Don't send bogus takedowns.** Knowingly misrepresenting that content is
  infringing carries liability. Only claim infringement of works you actually own
  or represent.
- **Fair use / exceptions** exist (criticism, commentary, parody, news, etc.) but
  are **narrow, fact‑specific, and not a blanket permission** — don't assume
  something is fair use just because it's non‑commercial or attributed.

## Beyond the US

- The **EU** has notice‑and‑takedown‑style obligations for hosting services
  (historically under the e‑Commerce Directive, now expanded by the **Digital
  Services Act**, which adds notice‑and‑action, transparency, and other duties).
- Many countries have analogous intermediary‑liability regimes.

If you operate internationally or host user content at scale, understand the
regimes that apply to your users, not just the US one.

## Practical takeaways

- **Hosting user content?** Set up a takedown process and (for US safe harbor)
  the agent + repeat‑infringer policy; reference it in your
  [terms of service](terms-of-service.md).
- **Publishing your own content?** Keep [license records](copyright-and-ip.md) so
  you can quickly answer any claim.
- **Someone copied you?** The takedown process is your fast, low‑cost remedy.
- **Received a notice?** Respond promptly and honestly; get advice before a
  counter‑notice.
